Confidence is free

Stating something firmly costs nothing and carries no risk of being shown wrong, because a general claim has no specific content to contradict. Firmness therefore conveys no information about accuracy, though it reliably affects how readers weigh a statement.

Specificity creates exposure

A source stating a number, a date and a method has made itself falsifiable. That exposure is the signal: a source willing to be shown wrong is behaving differently from one that is not, whatever either asserts.

Vagueness is usually a choice

Describing something as thoroughly tested rather than naming the tests is a decision about what to disclose. Occasionally there is a reason; more often the general version is the one that cannot be checked.

Look for the inconvenient detail

Sources that volunteer limitations, failures and uncertainties are doing something that costs them. The cost is what makes it informative, which is why a stated weakness raises credibility rather than lowering it.

Published method beats published conclusion

A conclusion asks for trust. A method invites repetition. Where both are available the method is the more valuable half, and it is the half most often omitted from summaries.

Check one thing, not everything

Verifying a single specific claim is usually quick and is strongly diagnostic. A source accurate on the one thing checked is more likely accurate elsewhere; one wrong on it has told you something important cheaply.

Hedging and precision are different

A source hedging everything is avoiding exposure. A source stating precisely what is known and precisely what is not is doing the opposite, and the two can read similarly if only the tone is noticed.

The practical standard

Prefer the source that makes it easiest to prove it wrong. That single preference does most of the work of judging reliability, and it needs no expertise in the subject.

Falsifiability is cheap to test

A claim that specifies a number, a date and a method can be checked in minutes by anyone. Picking a single specific claim and verifying it is a disproportionately efficient way to assess a source, because accuracy on checkable details correlates with care elsewhere.

Confidence is socially rewarded

Readers consistently rate assertive statements as more credible than hedged ones, independent of accuracy. Knowing that the bias exists is most of the defence against it, and it explains why the most reliable sources often read as the least impressive.
