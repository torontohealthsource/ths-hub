What is collected, specifically

A usable policy enumerates categories rather than asserting that information is collected to improve service. Specific lists allow a reader to decide; general statements do not, and the difference is usually deliberate.

Why, and on what basis

Collection should be tied to a stated purpose. Policies that collect for unspecified future purposes are reserving a right rather than describing a practice, and that reservation is the part worth noticing.

Who else receives it

Third-party processors, analytics providers and payment handlers all receive data. A policy naming categories of recipient is more useful than one naming none, and naming the actual services is more useful still.

Where it is stored

Jurisdiction determines which laws apply and who can compel disclosure. A policy silent on location leaves that unanswerable, which matters more for some readers than for others.

How long it is kept

Retention periods are among the least often stated and most consequential terms. Indefinite retention is a choice, and a policy that does not mention retention has usually made it.

What rights a reader has

Access, correction and deletion rights exist in many jurisdictions. A policy explaining how to exercise them is doing more than one that mentions they exist.

Changes without notice

Many policies reserve the right to change terms without notifying anyone. Where that clause exists, every other commitment in the document is conditional on it, which is worth reading first rather than last.

Why it is worth two minutes

The document is the only binding statement about what happens to information handed over. Two minutes spent on retention, recipients and the change clause covers most of what a reader would want to know.

Analytics and the data they carry

Most sites run third-party analytics that receive page addresses, approximate location and a device identifier on every visit. Whether that counts as personal information varies by jurisdiction, and a policy that mentions cookies without naming what the analytics provider receives has described the mechanism rather than the disclosure.

Payment data is usually handled elsewhere

Sellers typically never hold card numbers, because the payment processor does. That is a meaningful protection and it means the seller's policy is not the relevant document for payment data. A policy that explains this is more useful than one that implies greater custody than it has.

The change clause conditions everything else

Where a policy reserves the right to change terms without notice, every other commitment in it is provisional. That clause is worth reading first rather than last.

Retention is the least stated, most consequential term

How long information is kept determines how long it can be lost, requested or compelled. Policies silent on retention have usually chosen indefinite.

Third parties are where the data actually goes

Most policies permit sharing with processors, analytics providers and payment handlers. The list is often incorporated by reference rather than written out, which means the real set of parties holding the data is not stated anywhere a reader can see. Asking for the list is a legitimate request and the answer, or its absence, is informative.

Jurisdiction decides what rights exist

Where data is stored and which law governs the agreement determine whether a reader can demand access, correction or deletion at all. Two policies with identical wording can carry completely different enforceable rights depending on those two clauses.
