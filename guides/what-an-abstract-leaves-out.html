An abstract is a few hundred words chosen by the people whose result it describes. Reviewers see it, and it is the least constrained part of a paper, because it has room for conclusions and no room for the qualifications that support them.

Sample size is frequently absent

A large proportion of abstracts omit the number analysed, which bounds every claim that follows. Where it appears it is often the number enrolled rather than the number completing, and those diverge most in exactly the trials where it matters.

Spin is measurable

Studies comparing abstract conclusions with the results underneath find a consistent pattern of non-significant findings described in positive language. This is documented rather than alleged, and it is why the results section is worth the extra minute.

Absolute numbers are usually missing

Abstracts tend to report relative changes, which sound larger. The absolute figures, which determine whether anything matters practically, generally appear only in the tables.

Harms are under-reported there

Space pressure falls hardest on adverse effects. A trial reporting harms thoroughly in its body may mention none in the abstract, and a reader stopping there has seen only one side of the ledger.

Conference abstracts are weaker still

Work presented at a conference is often never published in full, and where it is, the final numbers frequently differ. Citing a conference abstract as a study overstates what exists.

What the abstract is good for

Deciding whether to read the paper, and establishing what question was asked. Those are real uses, and they are not the same as establishing what was found.

A reasonable rule

Never cite a finding from an abstract alone where the full text is available. If the full text is not available, say that the abstract is the source.
