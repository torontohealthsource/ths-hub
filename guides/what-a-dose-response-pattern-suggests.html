A graded relationship is hard to fake

If increasing an exposure produces a correspondingly larger effect, the pattern is difficult to explain by confounding alone, because a confounder would have to track the exposure across its whole range. This is why dose-response is one of the classical criteria for inferring causation from observation.

It is suggestive, not conclusive

A confounder that itself varies with the exposure will produce the same gradient. People taking more of something frequently differ systematically from people taking less, and the gradient then reflects that difference.

Not every real effect is graded

Some relationships are threshold effects: nothing below a level, full effect above it. Others are U-shaped, where too little and too much are both worse. The absence of a straight line is not evidence against causation.

Reverse gradients happen

Where people adjust their exposure in response to their condition, the sickest may take the most, producing a gradient that points the wrong way. Longitudinal data separate the two and cross-sectional data cannot.

Measurement error flattens it

Imprecisely measured exposure pulls an observed gradient toward flatness. A weak-looking dose-response may be a strong one measured badly, which is the opposite of the usual direction of bias and worth remembering.

Categories hide shape

Studies often split a continuous exposure into groups. Where the cut points were chosen after seeing the data, the resulting pattern can be an artefact of the splitting rather than of the relationship.

What strengthens it further

A gradient reproduced in independent populations, with different measurement methods, and a mechanism that predicts the shape. Those together move an association a long way toward a causal reading.

How to read a claim resting on it

Ask whether the gradient was predicted in advance, whether the exposure was measured more than once, and whether the extreme groups differ in anything else obvious.

Thresholds and U-shapes are real

Not every genuine relationship is a straight line. Some appear only above a level, and some worsen at both extremes. Absence of a clean gradient is therefore not evidence against causation, though it is often reported that way.
