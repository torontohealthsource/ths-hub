A cross-sectional study measures exposure and outcome in the same people at the same time.

Everything measured at once

A cross-sectional study measures exposure and outcome in the same people at the same time. It is fast and cheap and it produces a picture of a population at a moment, which is genuinely useful for some questions.

It is the right design for prevalence

How common something is in a population right now is exactly what this design answers, and no other design answers it as directly. Surveys of this kind underpin most public health planning.

Sequence is unavailable

Because both are measured together, the design cannot say which came first. An association is compatible with exposure causing outcome, outcome causing exposure, or both following something else entirely.

Prevalence favours survivors

People with a condition appear in the sample only if they still have it and are still available to be surveyed. Conditions that resolve quickly or end badly are under-represented, which distorts any comparison involving duration.

Response rates matter enormously

Who answers a survey differs from who does not, in ways usually related to the topic. A low response rate undermines a cross-sectional study more fundamentally than it undermines designs with internal comparisons.

Repeated cross-sections are not a cohort

Surveying a population repeatedly shows how the population changed. It does not show how individuals changed, and conclusions about individual trajectories from repeated snapshots are a known error.

Where it is the best available option

For questions about current distribution, for generating hypotheses cheaply, and for situations where following people forward is impossible. In those cases the alternative is usually no evidence at all.

How to read one

Take the prevalence figures seriously, treat any association as a hypothesis, and check the response rate before either.

Weighting makes a sample representative

Survey samples rarely match the population, so results are usually weighted to correct for who responded. The weights depend on assumptions about non-responders, and two defensible weighting schemes can produce noticeably different headline figures from identical responses.

Prevalence and incidence are different questions

Prevalence counts who has a condition now; incidence counts who develops it over a period. A cross-sectional design measures the first and cannot measure the second, and summaries routinely use the words interchangeably in ways that change the meaning substantially.
