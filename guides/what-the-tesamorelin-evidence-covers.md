# Approved for one narrow indication, used far outside it, and the gap between those two facts is the whole story

This molecule holds regulatory approval for a narrowly defined clinical indication in a specific patient group, supported by registered trials in that group.

## Approved, but for a specific population

This molecule holds regulatory approval for a narrowly defined clinical indication in a specific patient group, supported by registered trials in that group. That is a stronger evidence base than most items in this index and a narrower one than it is usually presented as.

## Off-label use is not covered by the approval

Approval establishes an acceptable benefit-to-risk balance for the studied population at the studied dose. People outside that population, at other doses, are not described by the trial data. The approval is frequently cited as though it generalises; it does not, and the trials were never designed to answer that question.

## A releasing hormone works indirectly

It acts by prompting the body's own release rather than supplying the end hormone, which means effects depend on the pathway being intact and responsive. That indirect route is why results vary more between individuals than a directly administered hormone would.

## What the assay settles

Identity and purity of the supplied material. For a molecule with a genuine approved comparator, provenance is the main thing separating research-supply material from the licensed product, which makes the independent report the most useful document available.

## The honest summary

Real trials, real approval, narrow scope. Anyone citing this compound's approval should be asked which indication and which population, because the answer is usually not the one the citation implies.

## Trial populations were specific

Where studies enrolled people with a defined condition, results describe that population. Extending them to healthy use is extrapolation beyond what any of the trials were designed to measure.

## Approved does not mean general

An authorisation names an indication. Reading approval as a broad endorsement is the most common error made with compounds that have any regulatory status at all.
