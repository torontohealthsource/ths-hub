The known material every percentage is measured against

A reference standard is the known material a measurement is compared against. Without one a figure is a reading rather than a measurement, and cross-lab comparison breaks.

A percentage needs a comparison

Purity is expressed relative to something. That something is a reference standard: material whose content is established to a stated accuracy by an authority that specialises in establishing it. Without one, an instrument reports proportions within a sample rather than how closely it matches what it claims to be.

Certified is a specific word

A certified reference material carries a documented value with a stated uncertainty, traceable to a national measurement institute. Material described as a standard without certification may be perfectly suitable and carries no such documentation, and the two are routinely described with the same word.

Traceability is the chain

The value on a certificate derives from a chain of comparisons reaching back to a primary standard. Traceability means that chain is documented. It is what allows two laboratories on different continents to produce comparable numbers, and it is the quiet infrastructure under every published percentage.

Standards degrade

Reference materials have expiry dates and storage requirements, and a standard used past either no longer carries its certified value. A laboratory's handling of its standards is part of what accreditation examines.

Why in-house standards are weaker

A laboratory can characterise its own material and use it as a reference. That is legitimate and the resulting numbers are internally consistent, but they are anchored to that laboratory rather than to a shared scale, which limits comparison with anyone else.

The uncertainty propagates

A reference with a stated uncertainty passes that uncertainty into every measurement made against it. A result reported to two decimal places against a standard certified to one is reporting more precision than the chain supports.

Why this is rarely published

Certificates of analysis seldom name the reference standard used. It is not usually concealment; it is a convention that assumes the reader is another laboratory. For a buyer it is a reasonable question to ask, and a laboratory that answers it is demonstrating something.

What it changes about reading a figure

A purity percentage is a comparison, and knowing what it was compared against turns it from a number into a measurement. Without that, two figures from different sources are not strictly comparable at all.
