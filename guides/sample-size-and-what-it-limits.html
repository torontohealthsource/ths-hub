How many participants a study had sets a ceiling on what it could possibly show

Sample size governs precision, not correctness. A small study is compatible with a wide range of true effects, which is why its striking headline figure is weaker evidence than it looks.

Small studies cannot detect small effects

Statistical power is the chance a study would find an effect if one genuinely exists. A study too small for its question will often report no difference, and that result describes the study rather than the world. Reporting it as evidence of absence is one of the most common errors in secondary coverage.

Small studies also exaggerate

When an underpowered study does produce a significant result, the effect it reports is usually larger than the truth, because only the larger random fluctuations clear the threshold. This is the main reason early dramatic findings shrink or vanish when someone attempts to replicate them.

Power calculations should be pre-stated

A well-run trial states, before enrolling anyone, how many participants it needs to detect an effect of a stated size. A paper without one may simply have stopped when the numbers looked favourable, and there is no way to tell from the published result alone.

Dropouts change the arithmetic

Enrolment is not the number analysed. Participants leave, and if they leave for reasons connected to the treatment, the group remaining is no longer the randomised one. The flow diagram showing how many were lost at each stage is often more informative than the results table.

Intention to treat, and why it looks unfair

Analysing participants in the group they were assigned to, even if they stopped or switched, usually dilutes the measured effect. It is still the correct primary analysis, because excluding non-compliers reintroduces exactly the selection randomisation was there to remove.

Stopping early inflates the effect

Trials monitored while running can stop when a difference reaches significance. Because random fluctuation is largest early, trials halted early for benefit systematically overstate it. Good trials pre-specify stopping rules and report how many interim looks were taken.

Why n matters more than p

Given the sample size and the effect size, the significance figure adds very little. Given only the significance figure, neither of the other two can be reconstructed, which is why the methods section is worth more than the abstract.

A reasonable reading habit

Find the number actually analysed before reading the conclusion. It bounds every claim that follows, and it usually sits in the methods rather than anywhere a summary would quote.

Cluster and repeated measures change the effective n

Observations that are not independent carry less information than their count suggests. Measuring the same person ten times is not ten independent observations, and neither is measuring ten people in one household. Analyses that ignore this report intervals that are too narrow, and the correction is routine but not universal.

Why post-hoc power calculations are uninformative

Calculating power after a study using its own observed effect adds nothing, because it is a deterministic function of the p-value already reported. A non-significant result will always yield low observed power, which is why the practice persists and why it answers no question the reader had.
