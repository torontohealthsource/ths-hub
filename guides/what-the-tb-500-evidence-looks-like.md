## It is a fragment, and that distinction gets lost

This item is a synthetic fragment of a larger naturally occurring protein. Much of the literature cited for it concerns the full protein, not the fragment, and the two are routinely discussed as though findings transfer. Whether they do is an open question, and a summary that cites the parent protein's research without saying so has overstated the case.

## Prohibited in competitive sport

It appears on the prohibited list maintained for competitive athletes, which is a straightforward factual matter anyone subject to testing needs to know before anything else about it. That status is independent of any question about effect.

## The human evidence is thin

As with several items here, the usable published work is largely preclinical. The absence of adequately powered human trials is the limit on every claim made about outcomes, and it is not resolved by the volume of informal reports.

## What our assays do establish

Identity and purity of the supplied material across a substantial number of batches. For a fragment, confirming the correct sequence matters more than usual, because a truncated or incorrect fragment would be a different molecule reported under the same name.

## Reading claims about it carefully

Check whether a cited study used the fragment or the parent protein, whether it was animal or human, and whether the outcome measured was clinical or a marker. Those checks remove most of what is confidently said about this compound.

## The published record for this item

The published set we counted contains 18 report(s) naming TB-500, averaging 99.25% purity. Each resolves on the testing laboratory's own portal rather than as a file hosted by the seller.

## Animal and in vitro dominate

Where the literature is overwhelmingly preclinical, the compound is best described as under investigation rather than studied. That is a statement about evidence depth and not a judgement about plausibility.

## Veterinary use is not human evidence

Use in animals under veterinary supervision establishes nothing about human dosing or safety, though it is frequently cited as though it did.
