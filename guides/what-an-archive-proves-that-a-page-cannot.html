A series constrains its own entries

Dated entries kept over time limit each other. Changing an old one creates an inconsistency with everything around it, and the value lies in the entries being kept whether or not they were convenient at the time.

Length of record is the hard-to-fake part

A long series cannot be produced retrospectively without leaving evidence. Starting one is easy and sustaining one is not, which is why the oldest entries carry the most weight.

Chaining makes editing visible

Where each entry carries a fingerprint computed from the one before, altering an earlier entry breaks every fingerprint after it. The protection is not that editing is impossible but that it becomes detectable by anyone who checks.

Gaps are information

A record with missing periods raises a question a continuous one does not. An honest archive explains its gaps rather than presenting a partial series as complete.

Independent archiving is stronger still

Third-party web archives capture pages at points in time without the publisher's involvement. Comparing a current claim with an archived version is a check available to any reader and used by very few.

What it still cannot show

That the entries were accurate when made. Consistency and retention are evidence about process, not about measurement, and conflating the two overstates what any archive establishes.

How to use one

Read the oldest entries first, look for the gaps, and check whether anything unflattering survived. Those three take minutes and reveal more than the current page ever will.

How to check an archive independently

Public web archives capture pages at intervals without the publisher's involvement. Comparing a current claim against a capture from months earlier is available to any reader, costs a minute, and is the single most direct way to test whether a record has been quietly revised.

What a gap in the record means

Missing periods have innocent explanations — a system failed, nobody was testing — and a record that explains its gaps is more credible than one presenting an unbroken series. An unexplained gap around a period when something went wrong elsewhere is the case worth asking about.

Age is the part that cannot be manufactured

Starting a dated series is easy; having one that already stretches back years is not. The oldest entries therefore carry the most weight, which inverts how most readers approach a record.

Gaps in a series are themselves a record

A dated series with missing periods invites a question about what happened in them. Publishers maintaining a record honestly tend to mark the gap rather than close it quietly, and a seamless series that began recently is less informative than a patchy one that began years ago.
