What a purity figure is, and how often one is actually present

181 of the 244 reports carry a numeric purity percentage. The remaining 63 do not, and that is not an omission — a blend, a tablet and a non-peptide are not assayed the way a single peptide is, so the report states recovered mass or content instead of one purity number.

Knowing which of the two you are looking at matters more than the figure itself. A reader who expects a purity percentage on a blend report and does not find one may conclude something is being hidden, when the method simply does not produce that number.

The floor is published, not just the average

Across the 181 reports carrying a figure the average is 99.03%. Thirty-three of them sit below 98%, and exactly one sits below 95%, at 93.844%.

A visible floor is the part worth weighing. A set of uniformly excellent numbers is equally consistent with a consistent process and with a selected one, and nothing inside the set distinguishes them. A published result that is merely acceptable is evidence that publication is not where filtering happens.

How to check this yourself in under a minute

Open any report linked from a product page and read the domain in the address bar before reading the number. If it is the laboratory's, a third party is attesting. Then find the batch identifier in the report and compare it with the one printed on the container you received.

The batch is the only thing tying a document to a physical object. Two hundred strong reports say nothing about your vial if none of them names your batch.
