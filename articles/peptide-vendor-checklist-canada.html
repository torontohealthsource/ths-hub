Start with the border, because it decides most outcomes

For a Canadian buyer the single highest-impact question is whether the parcel crosses a border. A domestic shipment moves on a national carrier with no customs entry, no brokerage charge, and no international facility able to hold it indefinitely. An inbound international parcel can be delayed, assessed, returned or simply never arrive, and none of that is within the seller's control once it is handed over.

So the first line of any Canadian checklist is: does this vendor ship domestically within Canada, and is the storefront I am on the Canadian one? Several operations run separate Canadian and US stores with different pricing, payment options and shipping rules, and the currency beside the total is the quickest way to confirm which one you are looking at.

Confirm the testing is checkable before you confirm anything else

Second line: can you open a laboratory report for the specific batch you will receive, on the laboratory's own domain rather than as a file served by the seller? That one question separates a documented supply chain from a storefront with attractive PDFs, and it takes under a minute.

A useful secondary check is whether the published set contains any unremarkable results. A record where every figure is excellent has usually been curated; one that includes weaker results alongside the strong ones is behaving like a record. The figures you want to see are a spread, not a line.

Price the things that are not in the price

Three costs routinely get left out of a first Canadian order. Shipping, which is often free above a threshold and chargeable below it — and note that a discount code reduces the subtotal the threshold is measured against, so a code can drop an order under it. Bacteriostatic water, which is sold separately and without which lyophilized compounds cannot be reconstituted at all. And the cost of being wrong, which on a final-sale policy is the full value of anything you cannot return.

That last one is why a first order from an unfamiliar vendor should be small even when bulk pricing is better. Multi-vial packs are commonly priced around 20% below buying the same count individually, and that is a genuine saving — but it is a commitment to ten units of something you have not yet verified for yourself.

Plan the receiving end before you order

Decide where the parcel will physically land. Canada Post Flex Delivery and PO boxes are widely supported and both avoid a parcel sitting at a door, which matters more in Canadian winters than people account for — temperature excursions on a doorstep are a real handling risk for a product that will be refrigerated after reconstitution.

Check storage requirements before delivery, not after. These usually differ by item within a single order: lyophilized compounds are typically stable at room temperature in transit and refrigerated for longer storage, while bacteriostatic water is stored at room temperature and never needs refrigeration. Assuming one rule covers the whole box is the most common handling mistake on a first order.

A first order, in the order it should happen

Put the checks in a sequence and most of the risk drops out. Confirm the storefront is the Canadian one and prices in Canadian dollars. Open a report for a product you intend to buy and confirm it resolves on the testing laboratory's own domain. Then add bacteriostatic water to the same order, decide the delivery destination before you pay rather than after, and check the cart total after any discount code against the free-shipping threshold. On arrival, match the batch code on the vial to the report before anything else — that is the last point at which checking still has consequences, because a final-sale policy means a problem found later is a problem you own.
