Purity and dose are two different tests, and most buyers conflate them

Across the 240 reports published for this vendor as of October 6, 2026, only 178 carry a numeric purity percentage. The other 62 are identity or content tests — they confirm what the material is, or how much of it is in the vial, and report a result without a percentage. That split is not a gap in the record; it is two different questions being asked of two different samples.

A purity figure is an area percentage from chromatography: of the material the instrument detected, this proportion was the target compound. It is silent on how much material was in the vial to begin with. A vial can assay at 99.6% purity and still be underfilled, and a vial can be filled exactly to label and assay at 96%. If you only ever read the purity number, you are reading one of the two tests that matter.

What the distribution looks like when you count every report instead of the headline

Counting all 178 percentages: the mean is 99.03% and the median is 99.52%, with a range from 93.844% to 99.99%. Ninety-five results (53.4%) land at or above 99.5%. The shape is the interesting part, and it is not a smooth curve. Just over half the set clusters tightly at the top, then the numbers thin out sharply, then a second group of thirty-two reappears in the 95–98% band. A distribution with two clusters rather than one tail is what an unfiltered record tends to look like, because different compounds and different synthesis runs do not behave identically. A single clean peak with nothing below it is the pattern that should prompt questions.

Why a result in the 95 to 98 band is not automatically a bad vial

In synthetic peptide work the material that is not the target compound is usually related to the synthesis itself: sequences missing a residue, truncated chains, and the water and counter-ion that come with a lyophilised powder. These are ordinary products of making the peptide, not contamination introduced afterwards, and their share moves with chain length. Longer and more difficult sequences routinely assay lower than short ones from the same facility on the same day.

That is why reading a 96% result as a defect, and a 99.6% result as proof of quality, usually misreads both. The question worth asking is whether the figure is consistent with the compound in question and whether it was published at all. Thirty-three of the reports in this set fall below 98% and are posted alongside the rest rather than removed, which tells you more about the record than any individual percentage in it does.

Reading a mass figure on a kit without raising a false alarm

Where a report gives a mass rather than a percentage, check what the figure covers before comparing it to the label. Six entries in this set are HGH kits, and the product name states the total for the kit while the report covers a single vial out of it. The ratio is consistent across all six at roughly ten to one, which is what a report on one vial of a ten-vial kit looks like.

Divide the reported mass by the kit total and you get about 10%, which reads as a vial holding a tenth of its labelled strength. Compare it instead to one vial's share and the six results land at 100.3%, 97.2%, 106.2%, 102.4%, 99.8% and 109.4% of label — four of the six at or above full strength. The alarming version of that number is an artifact of dividing by the wrong denominator, and we produced it internally before catching it. Match the unit on the report to the unit on the label first; a striking figure is a reason to check how it was derived, not a reason to repeat it.
