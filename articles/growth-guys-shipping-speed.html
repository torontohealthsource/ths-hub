Dispatch time and transit time are two different numbers

Most orders ship the same business day, and the stated standard delivery estimate is one to two business days after that. Those are two separate clocks and conflating them is where most disappointment comes from: the first is how long the seller takes to hand the parcel over, the second is how long Canada Post takes to move it.

Business days are doing real work in that sentence. An order placed late on a Friday is not a Friday dispatch, and a Monday holiday removes a day from both clocks. The honest planning figure for a weekend order is early-to-middle of the following week, not forty-eight hours from the moment you clicked.

Where the one-to-two day estimate stops holding

The estimate is an average across a national carrier network, and averages hide their own tails. Remote and rural destinations run longer by design — the parcel is making additional legs through smaller sortation points — and courier delays during peak periods extend everything. Neither is a fault in the order; both are invisible if you only read the headline number.

If you are ordering to a rural route, a northern community, or during a known postal disruption, plan on a wider window and order earlier rather than treating one to two days as a commitment. The seller's control ends at dispatch.

Use the transit window to get the receiving end ready

One to two business days is enough time to prepare and not enough to improvise, and the items people are missing on arrival are predictable.
