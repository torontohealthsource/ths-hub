Two different questions, routinely conflated

A purity assay asks what proportion of the material is the stated compound. It is typically run by high-performance liquid chromatography, and it is the figure most buyers mean when they say a result was ninety-nine percent.

An endotoxin assay asks something unrelated: whether bacterial residues are present. A sample can be extremely pure by chromatography and still carry endotoxin, because the two measurements are looking for different things. A high purity number is not a statement about sterility, and reading it as one is the most common mistake in this corner of the subject.

Why not every report carries every assay

Each assay costs money and takes instrument time, so testing records are usually assembled around whichever question the seller or the laboratory considered worth answering for that batch. The result is that a published set is rarely uniform.

In the Growth Guys set, 244 reports are published across 133 products and 181 of them carry a purity figure. That difference is the point: a report existing for a batch does not tell you which assays it contains. The only way to know is to open the document and read the headings.

How to read a report for this specifically

Find the batch number on the packaging and locate that batch on the laboratory's own portal rather than on a page the seller controls. Then read the assay headings before the numbers, because the headings are what tell you which question was asked.

If an endotoxin result is present it will be named as such and carry its own units, separate from the purity percentage. If it is absent, the honest conclusion is that this batch was not tested for it — not that it passed, and not that it failed.
