A blend report answers a different question, and the numbers show it

Twenty-four reports in the published set cover blends, across ten distinct blend names — among them KLOW, GLOW, BPC-157 with TB-500, CJC-1295 with Ipamorelin, and Semax with Selank. Exactly one of those twenty-four carries a numeric purity percentage.

That is not a documentation failure. It is what the method produces. A blend contains more than one active compound, so there is no single substance for a purity percentage to describe.

What the report states instead

It states recovered mass for the combination. Batch BPCTB10, a 10mg/10mg blend, reports 20.39mg. Batch BPCTB09, a 6mg/6mg blend, reports 11.74mg. KLO06 reports 97.47mg and GLO06 reports 87.43mg.

So the question a blend COA can settle is whether the stated quantity is present. The question it cannot settle is how the total divides between the components, unless the report specifically assays them separately.

Why that distinction decides what you can conclude

Two vials can both report the correct combined mass and contain quite different ratios of the two compounds. Combined mass is a real check and a useful one, but it is a weaker claim than a per-compound purity, and it should not be quoted as if it were the same thing.

The same compound makes this visible within one catalogue. Ipamorelin on its own is reported at 99.82% to 99.89% purity across four single-compound reports; the four CJC-1295-with-Ipamorelin blend reports carry no purity figure at all. Same peptide, different document, because the product is different.

What to ask of a blend before buying it

Confirm the batch on your vial appears in a report. Read the combined mass against the combined label. Then decide whether combined mass is enough for your purpose, because that is the honest limit of the document.

If per-component figures matter to you, the single-compound products are where the published record is strongest. That is a real trade-off between convenience and evidence, and it is better made knowingly.
