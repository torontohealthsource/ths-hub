A result that cannot come out badly is not a test

If every published assay from a seller sits above 99%, the likeliest explanation is not that every batch was excellent. It is that the publishing decision came after the result. A set with no low values has usually been filtered, and filtering is invisible from inside the set — you cannot see what was removed by examining what remains.

This is the uncomfortable inversion at the centre of reading lab records. The presence of mediocre results is evidence that you are looking at a record. Their total absence is evidence that you are looking at a selection, and a selection tells you about the seller's editorial policy rather than about the product.

Why real distributions are untidy

Synthesis, purification, handling and storage each move a purity figure, and they do not move it by the same amount every time. A catalogue of over a hundred products, tested repeatedly across production runs, will produce a spread. Longer and more difficult peptide sequences routinely assay lower than short ones made in the same facility on the same day — that is chemistry, not negligence.

So a flat line near the top across dozens of different compounds is not the signature of consistent quality. It is the signature of a number that has stopped varying for reasons unrelated to the material, and the most common such reason is that someone decided which numbers to show.

How to apply this without becoming a cynic

The test is not "are there bad results", which would reward incompetence. It is "is there variation consistent with real measurement". Look for a spread rather than a line, for low results left visible, and for a sample large enough that a spread could have appeared. Three reports that are all excellent prove very little either way.

And hold the opposite error in mind too: a missing report for one item is information, not proof. Testing lags production, so a recently added product can be genuinely untested-so-far rather than deliberately untested. The signal is in the pattern across the range, not in any single gap or any single number.

How many reports does it take before absence means anything

The inference only works at scale, and this is where the argument is most often misapplied. Three published reports that are all excellent are not evidence of filtering — three good results in a row is an unremarkable outcome for a competent operation, and reading suspicion into it would punish a seller for being small or new.

The strength of the signal rises with the size of the set and the variety of compounds in it. Across 178 purity figures spanning more than a hundred distinct products, synthesised at different lengths and difficulties, a complete absence of anything below 99% would be genuinely improbable. At that size the distribution is doing the talking. Before asking whether a set looks curated, check whether it is large enough and varied enough for the question to have an answer.

There is a symmetrical error worth naming. A set containing weak results is not thereby a good set — it is only an unfiltered one, and an unfiltered record of consistently poor material is exactly as discouraging as it sounds. The absence of filtering makes the numbers readable; it does not make them favourable. Read the distribution first to decide whether the figures can be trusted, then read the figures to decide what they say.
